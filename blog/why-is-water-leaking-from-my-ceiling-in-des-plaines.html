Why is water leaking from my ceiling in Des Plaines

Water on a ceiling can come from the roof, plumbing, or condensation — and the source decides the trade. This guide explains the six roof-related causes and how to narrow the source.

W
WP Exterior IncNovember 2026 · 9 min read

Water leaking from a ceiling is alarming, and the first question is not “how bad is it?” but “where is it coming from?” Roof leaks, plumbing leaks, and condensation all show on ceilings, and each belongs to a different trade. This guide explains the six roof-related causes, how to narrow the source, what ceiling damage means, and when to call — without repair instructions.

If water is entering now, interior control comes first: protect belongings, catch drips, note the time and weather, and photograph the stain’s position. Do not open the ceiling or climb into a wet attic.

The short answer: is it the roof?

A ceiling leak is likely roof-related if it follows rain or snowmelt, appears or worsens during storms, and sits below or downhill from a roof feature — a valley, a chimney, a skylight, or a penetration. It is more likely plumbing if it appears without rain, follows fixture use, or sits below a bathroom or kitchen. Condensation shows as dampness or staining without a clear weather pattern, often in winter, near poorly vented attics.

Those are tendencies, not diagnoses. The source is established by checking the pattern against weather and by inspection — not assumed from a single stain.

Why water is leaking from a ceiling in Des Plaines IL

Six roof-related causes, in the order they are usually checked:

1. Flashing failures

Chimney, wall, valley, and skylight flashing are the roof’s most stressed seals. When flashing lifts, rusts, or separates, water enters at the joint and travels along the deck before showing on a ceiling — often far from the entry point.

2. Pipe boots and penetrations

Rubber boots around vent pipes crack with age and UV exposure, and the joint where a penetration meets the roof opens. Pipe-boot failure is a common cause of ceiling stains below bathrooms, and it is easily confused with a plumbing leak — the weather pattern distinguishes them.

3. Shingle damage

Missing, lifted, or bruised shingles let water under the covering. Hail bruising and wind lifting are the storm causes; age and granule loss are the slow causes. The stain’s position relative to slopes and features narrows the search.

4. Gutters and ice dams

Blocked gutters and ice dams back water up under the roof edge, and the resulting stains appear near exterior walls — the classic ice-dam signature. The ice-dam mechanism (attic heat, deck warming, refreeze at the eave, backup) is explained in the winter guide on this blog.

5. Condensation

Warm, moist air from bathrooms and kitchens that vents into the attic, or insufficient attic ventilation, condenses on cold surfaces and shows as dampness or staining. Condensation is an attic and ventilation question, not a roof-covering question — and it is a separate diagnosis from a leak. See the roof ventilation installation page.

6. Skylight and chimney joints

Skylight curbs and chimney joints are large penetrations with complex flashing. Leaks at either show on ceilings below or beside them, and the diagnosis must separate flashing failure from the unit itself. Skylight topics are on the skylight installation page.

How to narrow the source of a ceiling leak

Narrowing the source is a record-keeping exercise before it is an inspection exercise:

  1. Note the weather pattern. Does the leak follow rain, snowmelt, wind-driven rain, or no weather at all? The pattern is the strongest single clue.
  2. Note the position. Where is the stain relative to exterior walls, chimneys, skylights, valleys, and bathrooms above? Photograph with dates.
  3. Check the attic safely. From a safe position, look for daylight, staining, damp insulation, and water paths — without climbing into a wet attic or disturbing insulation more than needed.
  4. Check what is above. A bathroom or kitchen above the stain suggests plumbing; a roof feature above suggests roofing; neither is conclusive alone.
  5. Call the right trade. Roof-related signs point to a roofer; plumbing signs point to a plumber; condensation signs point to ventilation and insulation questions. If the source is unclear, say so when calling — a roofer can rule roofing in or out.

For inspection scope, see the roof inspection page.

What a roof leak does to a ceiling

A ceiling stain is the visible end of a longer path: water enters the roof, travels along the deck or through insulation, soaks the ceiling material, and shows as a stain, sag, or drip. By the time water shows, insulation and decking may already be wet — which is why the source matters more than the stain.

Ceiling and interior repair — drywall, paint, insulation — is a separate trade from roofing. A roofer stops the water; interior repair restores the ceiling. Ask who handles each part, and get the roofing scope in writing before work starts. Do not paint over a stain until the source is fixed and the area is dry.

Common mistakes with ceiling leaks

  • Assuming the stain marks the entry point. Water travels along decks and under membranes. The entry point is often uphill or sideways from the stain.
  • Painting over the stain. Paint hides the record and traps moisture. Fix the source, dry the area, then repair the ceiling.
  • Opening the ceiling. Cutting into a wet ceiling without knowing what is above risks electrical, plumbing, and structural surprises. Leave it to the relevant trade.
  • Waiting for the next rain. Leaks worsen with freeze–thaw and repeated wetting. Document and call after the first sign.
  • Calling the wrong trade first. The weather pattern distinguishes roof from plumbing in most cases. Record it before calling.

When to call a professional

Call promptly when water is entering, when a stain is growing, when the ceiling sags, or when the source is unclear. State the weather pattern, the stain’s position, and when it first appeared.
