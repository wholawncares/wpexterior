Why are my shingles curling and cracking

Curling, cracking, and clawing are different patterns with different causes. This guide explains the six causes, what the pattern tells you, and how the repair-or-replace decision is made.

W
WP Exterior IncNovember 2026 · 8 min read

Curling shingles lift at the edges; cracking shingles split through the mat; clawing shingles cup downward in the middle. Those are different patterns with different causes, and reading the pattern correctly matters — because the right response ranges from monitoring to replacement, and the wrong one wastes money or misses a failing roof.

This guide explains the six causes, what the pattern tells you, how storm damage differs from aging, and how the repair-or-replace decision is made. No lifespan figure here is a warranty, and no repair instruction is given.

Why are my shingles curling and cracking in Des Plaines IL?

Curling and cracking are the visible signs of a shingle losing its flexibility and its granule surface. The causes are usually a combination — age plus heat, or ventilation plus installation — and the pattern across the roof is more informative than any single shingle.

Des Plaines’ housing stock makes this a common question: most homes were built before 1980 (median year built 1966, ACS 2019–2023), and many roofs are on their second or third covering. Structure age is not roof age, but the pattern of aging roofs on mid-century houses is the context for most curling questions.

The six causes of curling and cracking on Des Plaines roofs

Six causes recur in guides, usually in combination:

1. Age and granule loss

Shingles lose granules with age and weather. As the asphalt mat is exposed, it dries, shrinks, and cracks. Guide lifespans (attributed, not warranties): 3-tab 12–20 years, architectural 20–30 years (XL guide). A roof near the end of its guide lifespan with widespread curling is a replacement question, not a repair question.

2. Poor attic ventilation

Heat trapped in the attic bakes shingles from below, accelerating aging and curling. The Des Plaines guide’s ventilation shorthand is 1:150, or 1:300 with roof and soffit vents — a shorthand that omits the IRC’s balance and distance conditions, so it is not the full rule. Ventilation topics are on the roof ventilation installation page.

3. Hail and wind damage

Hail bruises the mat and knocks off granules; wind lifts and creases shingles. Storm damage is usually localised to the storm-facing slopes, while aging is spread across the roof. The hail guide on this blog explains the difference in detail.

4. Ice dams and freeze-thaw

Freeze–thaw cycling — about 121 freezing nights a year at O’Hare, 1991–2020 normals — works on shingle seals and flashing joints. Ice-dam backup lifts shingle edges at the eave and soaks the deck edge, accelerating curling at the lowest courses.

5. Installation errors

Shingles installed without proper nailing, alignment, or underlayment curl early. Installation errors usually show as a pattern — a slope, a section, or a repeating defect — rather than scattered age wear. The pattern is the diagnostic clue.

6. Tree cover and debris

Shade and debris keep shingles damp, encouraging moss and algae growth and accelerating granule loss. Overhanging branches drop debris that traps moisture. Trimming that can be done safely from the ground is homeowner work; roof-level tree work belongs to a tree professional.

What the pattern tells you: curling vs cracking vs clawing

The three patterns point in different directions:

  • Curling (edges lift upward). Usually age, heat, or ventilation. Widespread curling suggests the covering is near the end of its service life.
  • Cracking (splits through the mat). Usually age, freeze–thaw, or hail bruising that has dried and split. Cracked shingles no longer shed water reliably.
  • Clawing (middle sinks, edges stay). Often an installation or decking issue — the shingle conforms to an uneven or deteriorating surface beneath it. Clawing in a pattern suggests a substrate question for inspection.

Location matters too: eave curling suggests ice-dam and freeze–thaw effects; ridge curling suggests heat and age; slope-wide curling suggests ventilation or age; localised curling suggests storm or installation causes. An inspection reads the pattern across the whole roof, not one shingle.

Shingle damage after a storm in Des Plaines Illinois

After a storm, the question is whether damage is storm-caused or age-caused — a distinction that matters for insurance and for scope. Storm damage is usually localised to the storm-facing slopes, with bruising, creasing, or missing tabs; aging is spread across the roof, with uniform curling and granule loss.

Documentation matters: dated photographs of the damage, the storm date, and the pattern across slopes. For the combined hail-and-wind topic, see the storm damage roof repair page, and for inspection scope, the roof inspection page.

Repair, watch, or replace: how the decision is made

The decision follows the pattern, not the symptom:

  • Watch. A few curled shingles on a young roof, with no leaks and sound flashing, may be monitored — with dated photographs and a re-check after the next season.
  • Repair. Localised damage — a section, a slope, or a flashing detail — with the rest of the roof sound may be repaired in kind. See the roof repair page.
  • Replace. Widespread curling, cracking, and granule loss across multiple slopes, especially on a roof near the end of its guide lifespan, points to replacement. See the roof replacement page.

No repair instruction is given here. Re-seating, sealing, or replacing shingles is roofer work; the homeowner’s role is to record the pattern and call. Tear-off rules apply at replacement: two or more existing layers, a water-soaked or deteriorated base, or a shake/slate/tile base requires tear-off, and at most two layers may exist on completion.

Common mistakes homeowners make with worn shingles in Des Plaines

  • Sealing curled shingles with caulk. Caulk is not a shingle repair. It traps moisture and masks the pattern an inspector needs to read.
  • Replacing one slope and ignoring ventilation. If poor ventilation caused the curling, a new slope over the same attic will curl the same way.
  • Assuming a new roof fixes an attic problem. The attic is part of the roof system. Ventilation and air sealing are addressed with the covering, not after it.
  • Waiting for a leak. Curling and cracking are early warnings. By the time water shows inside, decking and insulation may already be affected.

When to call a professional

Call when curling or cracking is widespread, when leaks have appeared, when the pattern suggests a substrate or ventilation question, or when the roof is near the end of its guide lifespan. An inspection establishes whether the answer is watch, repair, or replace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are my shingles curling in Des Plaines IL?

Age and granule loss, poor attic ventilation, hail and wind, ice dams and freeze–thaw, installation errors, and tree cover — usually in combination. The pattern across the roof (widespread vs localised, eave vs ridge) points to the cause.

Can curling shingles be repaired in Des Plaines?

Localised curling on an otherwise sound roof may be repaired in kind. Widespread curling across multiple slopes, especially near the end of the roof’s guide lifespan, points to replacement. An inspection establishes which applies.

How long do shingles last in Des Plaines Illinois?

Guide lifespans, attributed and not warranties: 3-tab 12–20 years, architectural 20–30 years (XL guide). Actual life depends on installation, ventilation, hail and wind exposure, and maintenance.

Do curling shingles mean I need a new roof in Des Plaines?

Not always. A few curled shingles on a young roof may be monitored; widespread curling and cracking across slopes usually means replacement. The pattern, the roof’s age, and inspection findings decide.
